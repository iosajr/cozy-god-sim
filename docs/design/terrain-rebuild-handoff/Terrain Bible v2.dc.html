01 · Diagnosis

Why the probe reads as confetti

Four specific things in scripts/terrain_style_probe.gd. None of them is a taste problem — they're all missing stages.

Quantise with no smoothing pass
_quantize_level maps noise straight to one of five levels, per cell, and stops. Simplex at frequency = 0.008 over 4 m cells gives features about 30 cells wide — but the per-cell quantisation shatters them at every boundary. Every tier edge is a ragged one-cell fringe.
No region concept at all
Nothing knows a plateau exists, so nothing can cull a tiny one, guarantee it access, or give it a biome. Single-cell spikes and one-cell notches survive to the mesh. This is the change that matters most.
Cliffs are box columns, one flat albedo
_build_cliff_multimesh pushes a scaled BoxMesh per cell at Color(0.72, 0.55, 0.25) — 16,384 boxes, hard 90° corners, no strata, no lip, no shadow line. The most-looked-at surface in the game has the least art on it.
Uniform trees, one sphere
_build_trees picks 1,200 uniform random cells and puts an identical SphereMesh canopy on each. Even density averages out to texture; identical silhouettes read as a repeating stamp; and trees land right on cliff lips, which is what hides the tiering.
Worth saying: the probe is doing its job. It is labelled disposable look-dev, the camera rig is already the real one, and the constants are well chosen. Keep cell_size = 4.0 and terrace_step = 1.5; everything below is built on them.
02 · The continent

Shape first, height second

The land mask is generated before any terracing. Get an interesting coastline and the terraces inherit interesting shapes for free — this is why the current probe, which terraces an infinite plane, has nothing to hang variety on.

MASK RECIPE
1
Radial falloff from centre — guarantees a single landmass, no stray specks.
2
Subtract low-frequency ridged noise from the falloff — this is what cuts the deep bays.
3
Add three or four hand-placed peninsula blobs so the silhouette has intent, not just noise.
4
Threshold, then majority-smooth twice. Discard any island under 400 cells.
5
Distance-to-coast field. Terrace count rises with it, so mountains are inland by construction.
SIZE
512 × 512 cells at 4 m — a 2 km continent. Big enough for your five-or-six-cities target, small enough to hold in one mesh set while you judge the look.
BIOME PLACEMENT
You place four seed points by hand in the editor. Each cell takes the nearest seed, weighted by a noise field so borders wander instead of drawing Voronoi lines. Snow additionally requires tier ≥ 5, so the mountain biome finds the mountains.
THE ONE HARD RULE
Biomes never change the tier field. They only change palette, cliff treatment, foliage and step prop. Terrain shape and art direction stay separable — otherwise every biome tweak becomes a regeneration.

Per agressive mountains: drop the three hard strata bands for one warm grey-brown mass with a pale rim on the top 7% of the face and soft vertical column divisions. Reads softer, closer to your reference, and costs one less shader term.
SOFTER CORNERS
Two mechanisms, both cheap. In the field: demote one-cell spikes, fill one-cell notches, break diagonal-only touches. In the mesh: chamfer strip on every convex corner, single fillet triangle on every concave one. Neither smooths the tier away.
NEVER
A cliff under 4 cells of run. Two tiers of drop in one lowland face. A tier edge with a tree on it.
04 · Access

Two props, one guarantee

The solver decides where; the biome and the settlement mask decide what. I have deliberately dropped the grass ramp from lowland terrain — with slab and stair both present it muddies the language, and it reappears only as the mountain switchback tread.

Rock slab steps — the wild default
Three or four irregular slabs, each rotated a few degrees, jutting from the face with deep shadow between them. Scribed dark top edge in the Don't Starve manner. This is the one from your third screenshot, and it is the default everywhere no settlement exists.
Built stairs — settlements only
Even risers, pale stone, low kerb either side, snapped hard to the grid. Placed where the settlement mask is set, so a village visibly civilises its terrain as it grows. Legible from the furthest camera zoom — that legibility is the point.
The guarantee. Regions form a graph; an edge exists where a region at tier n shares a straight border run of ≥3 cells with a region at tier n−1. Union-find from the tier-0 set; while any region is unreachable, place the cheapest missing access — longest, straightest runs first, because those make the best-looking steps. Above tier 4 the solver switches from placing a prop to carving a gully. No region is meshed before it has a route to sea level, and a test asserts this across 200 seeds.
05 · Mountains

Tall faces, gully routes, jagged summit

The profile B mountain form — double-height faces, narrow ledges — with the switchback dropped. You didn't like the zig-zag, and you were right: a staircase cut across a 3 m rock face is a built object on a wild mountain, and it fights the silhouette that makes the form work. So access comes off the face altogether.

Double riser
Above tier 4, faces drop two terraces at once — 3.0 m of unbroken rock. Too tall to step, so a bare face genuinely stops you. This is what makes the mountain read alpine rather than like more stairs.
Gullies, not staircases
Where a peak needs a route, the generator carves one: a 2-cell channel cut back into the massif that steps down through the tiers, floored with tumbled slabs and scree. You climb through the mountain's own weakness rather than over its face. Nothing is bolted on — the route is terrain.
Saddles between peaks
Wherever two peaks sit within about 30 cells, the field between them is held two tiers lower — a pass. Passes are the primary way up a range, gullies the secondary way up a single peak. Both give you a landmark to walk toward, which a zig-zag never did.
Narrow ledges
Minimum region area drops from 260 cells to 60 above tier 4, so ledges 3–5 cells wide survive the absorb pass. Peaks reach tier 8–12 in the footprint a lowland plateau would use.
Jagged unbuildable summit
The top two tiers skip the smoothing and chamfer passes entirely — raw quantised noise, which is exactly the confetti we removed elsewhere, and up there it reads as shattered rock. Flagged unbuildable; is_walkable still returns true so you can stand and look.
Snow line. Not a height threshold — a blend. Snow coverage ramps from 0 at tier 5 to full at tier 8, modulated by a noise field so it pools in hollows and blows off exposed edges. Don't Starve's winter palette: near-white tops, blue-grey rock faces, ink-black crevices, almost no saturation. The one place in the game where the greens disappear.
If gullies aren't it either — two other ways to keep the tall faces and lose the zig-zag: a tunnel mouth at the foot that emerges on a high ledge (strong landmark, but hides the climb), or rockfall ramps, a talus cone piled against one face high enough to walk up (most natural, but softens the silhouette you liked). Say the word and I'll swap the section.
06 · Water

Sea below, lakes above, falls between

One global plane plus per-basin fills. The falls are not authored — they fall out of the fill algorithm, which is the whole reason to do it this way.

Sea, tier 0
A single plane, as you already have. Everything outside the land mask is sea; the coast gets a 2-cell sand hem where the mask meets it.
Perched lakes
Flood-fill each depression from its lowest cell upward. If it fills without reaching a region border, it's a lake, sitting at whatever tier its basin is on.
Spillways
If the fill reaches a border, the lowest border cell is the outlet. Water pours over the cliff there as a fall, and the stream continues on the tier below. Falls appear where they should because the terrain put them there.
Art note. Two water materials only — sea and fresh. Fresh water is lighter and greener, near-opaque with a pale rim where it meets the shore, because transparent water in a stylised world just shows you the mesh you were trying to hide. Falls get a soft white column plus a spray card at the foot; no simulation.

08 · Foliage

Ghibli canopies, not spheres

Three silhouettes, clumped by a second noise field, with a hard two-value split on every canopy — sunlit top, shadowed underside, no gradient between. The rule that does most of the work: density is zero within 2 cells of a cliff edge. Clear lips are what make the tiering readable.

A · BROAD — 60%
Wide flattened crown built from three overlapping card clusters, slightly asymmetric. Meadow, paddy edges, coast. The workhorse.
B · TALL — 25%
Narrow cedar mass, darkest value in the scene. Punctuates ridge lines, dominant in deep forest, snow-laden on mountains.
C · SCRUB — 15%
Low bush cluster, no trunk. Breaks up cliff feet and talus, hides the mesh seam where wall meets ground.
Against your current scatter. Keep the MultiMesh approach and the per-species instance split — that part is right. Replace uniform random cell picking with a clump field, add the edge exclusion, and give each instance a random yaw plus ±15% scale. Three meshes and those three rules will change the look more than any shader in this document.

10 · Integration

It slots in behind Terrain

Your docs/systems/view-camera-terrain.md already decided this: height at a point, walkable or not, and nothing may assume flat. That decision is what makes this rebuild cheap — the tier field answers both questions in constant time, and nothing above the interface changes.

class_name TerracedTerrain
extends Terrain
## The terraced continent behind the Terrain interface. Owns the tier field;
## answers height and walkability from it without touching the mesh.

var _field: PlateauField          # tier, region, biome, flags per cell
var _cell_size: float = 4.0       # matches terrain_style_probe
var _terrace_step: float = 1.5


func height_at(x: float, z: float) -> float:
	var cell: Vector2i = _to_cell(x, z)
	if not _field.inside(cell):
		return 0.0
	return float(_field.tier_at(cell)) * _terrace_step


## False on a cliff face, false in deep water, true on any ledge top and on
## the footprint of a step or switchback flight.
func is_walkable(x: float, z: float) -> bool:
	var cell: Vector2i = _to_cell(x, z)
	if not _field.inside(cell):
		return false
	if _field.is_water(cell) and not _field.is_shallow(cell):
		return false
	return true


func _to_cell(x: float, z: float) -> Vector2i:
	return Vector2i(floori(x / _cell_size), floori(z / _cell_size))

12 · Order of work

Five sittings, each one shippable

SITTING 1 · THE FIELD, IN 2D
Land mask, terracing, smoothing, region labelling — with a debug TextureRect painting tier as colour. Judge coastline and plateau shapes flat before any 3D. Ten minutes tells you whether it reads.
SITTING 2 · ACCESS & THE TEST
Region graph, union-find, step sites drawn as dots on the same debug map. Then the 200-seed reachability test. Do this before meshing — it is the thing most likely to force a field change.
SITTING 3 · MESH & THE CLIFF
Chunked ArrayMesh with chamfer strips, plus the cliff and ground shaders. One biome only, no props, no trees. Get the edge right in isolation — everything else is dressing on it.
SITTING 4 · WATER & MOUNTAINS
Basin fill, spillways, falls; then the mountain parameter set and the snow blend. Both are variations on stages you already have, which is why they come after the base look is settled.
SITTING 5 · DRESSING & THE OTHER THREE BIOMES
Foliage clumping, step and stair props, sand hem, biome palettes wired to shader uniforms. By this point a biome is a data row, not a code change — which is the test of whether the earlier sittings were built right.
TWO THINGS I'D STILL DECIDE BEFORE SITTING 1
Whether players terraform tiers at runtime — it decides chunk size and whether the mesher must run incrementally. And whether settlements are placed before or after terrain: placing them first lets the solver put built stairs where villages will actually be, which is a much stronger read than retrofitting them.
