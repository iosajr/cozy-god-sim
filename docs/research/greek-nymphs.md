# Greek nymphs

What the mythology actually says, since the world's second species is one.

## Dryad is the right word

Dryad means tree nymph. It comes from *drys* (δρῦς), "oak", and was first
used of oak nymphs before widening to cover trees generally. "Tree nymph"
is the plain-English gloss of the same thing, not a correction of it.

## They are female, and they are not genderless

*Nymphe* (νύμφη) means "young woman" or "bride" — it is still the ordinary
modern Greek word for a bride. Every class of nymph in the sources is
female. Nothing supports a genderless reading.

They are divine but not immortal. A nymph is long-lived and can die.

## Satyrs are not male dryads

Satyrs are male woodland spirits, part-goat or part-horse, companions of
Dionysus. They share the woods with nymphs and appear beside them
constantly in art, which is where the pairing comes from — but they are a
different kind of creature, not the male half of one species. There are no
male nymphs.

Female satyrs exist only as a Renaissance invention, not in Greek sources.

## Hamadryads are the ones bound to a tree

- A **dryad** lives among trees and is long-lived.
- A **hamadryad** is born together with one particular tree and dies when
  that tree dies.

The Meliai, ash nymphs, are born from Gaia after she is made fertile by
the blood of the castrated Ouranos. The sources are not cosy.

## The classes are a domain, not a species

The useful part. Nymphs are sorted by what they belong to, not by what
they are:

- **Trees** — Dryades, Hamadryades, Meliai (ash), Meliades (fruit),
  Daphnaiai (laurel)
- **Fresh water** — Naiades, split again into springs, fountains, rivers,
  lakes and marshes
- **Sea** — Nereides; **ocean** — Okeanides
- **Land** — Oreiades (mountains), Alseides (groves), Napaiai (dells)
- **Sky** — Hesperides (evening), Hyades (rain), Pleiades (stars)

The classes overlap freely: the nymph of a tree growing beside a spring is
both a dryad and a naiad.

## Confidence

The gender, the etymology, the hamadryad binding and the satyr distinction
are consistent across every source read. The domain classification is
consistent too.

Which class owns the oak is not: some sources make dryads the original oak
nymphs, others give the oak to the hamadryads. Treat the tree-by-tree
mapping as decorative rather than settled.

Theoi is the strongest source here and its pages refused direct fetching,
so its content came through search excerpts rather than the pages
themselves.
